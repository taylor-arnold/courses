The data is the corpus of 75 authors from the last chapter. One table has the daily page views for each author in 2025, and the other has the 500 most recent edits to each author's article, timestamped to the second. On a sheet of paper, hand write answers to the following.

  1. Say what hidden assumption makes the row-number axis of the page-view plot work, and what would make it start lying.
  2. Say what the Date type gives you that plotting against a row number or a fractional year cannot.
  3. The weekday averages suggest a particular kind of reader for Chaucer, so say what evidence would be needed to actually establish that, since the table records only counts and no readers.
  4. We collected only the 500 most recent edits per page, so give a comparison between two authors that this quietly ruins.
  5. Calling .dt.convert_time_zone() on the naive timestamps gives the right answer on this data anyway, so explain why getting the right answer can be the problem.

Solutions

1. The hidden assumption behind the row-number axis, and what would break it.

The row number stands in for the date only if the data has exactly one row per
day, sorted in calendar order, with no days missing. Then row 45 really is the
45th day of the year and the spacing between rows is one day everywhere.

The axis starts lying as soon as any part of that fails:

  - Missing days. The source leaves out days on which a page had no views, so
    some of the quieter pages are already missing a few. After a gap, every
    later row sits one or more days to the left of where it belongs, and two
    points that are a week apart get drawn next to each other.
  - A different sort order. Sort by views, or forget to sort after a join, and
    the row number no longer follows time at all.
  - Numbering before filtering. If the row index is added to the whole table
    and the filter comes after, Chaucer's rows are numbered by their position
    among all 75 authors.

None of these raise an error. The plot still draws a clean line, which is why
the assumption is hard to notice.

2. What the Date type gives you that a row number or fractional year cannot.

A Date knows it is a date on the calendar. That gives three things.

First, readable axes. plotnine labels a Date axis with real dates, and
scale_x_date() lets us choose the breaks and format ("1 month", "%Y-%m"). A
fractional year like 2025.12 has to be explained to the reader, and a row
number cannot be read as a date at all.

Second, calendar structure. The .dt namespace pulls out the weekday, month,
day of year, and quarter. The weekday is the clearest case: neither a row number
nor a fractional year can tell you that a day was a Tuesday without extra
work. This is also how we found that Chaucer's tallest spike was 14 February.

Third, exact arithmetic and comparison. A Date handles months of different
lengths and leap years correctly, where the fractional-year formula pretends
every month has 31 days. Dates can be filtered against pl.date(2025, 2, 14)
and do not depend on the table being sorted or complete, unlike the row number.

3. What evidence would be needed to establish that Chaucer's readers are students doing coursework.

There is no single correct answer. A good response says that the weekday
pattern is only consistent with student readers, then names evidence that could
tell student readers apart from other explanations.

The table counts page loads. It has no information about who loaded the page
or why, so a weekday dip on Saturdays fits many stories: students, office
workers browsing at their desks, teachers preparing lessons, or automated
traffic that runs on a working-week schedule.

Evidence that would move the claim forward, from weaker to stronger:

  - The academic calendar in the counts. If the page is read for coursework,
    views should fall in July and August and over the winter break, and rise
    when terms start. The same weekday dip should be weaker or absent in
    those months.
  - Comparison authors. An author who is rarely taught should show a weaker
    weekday pattern than Chaucer. If every page in the corpus shows the same
    dip, the pattern is about Wikipedia traffic in general and not about
    Chaucer's readers.
  - Data about the readers themselves, which this table does not have: where
    visits were referred from (course websites or learning platforms), the
    places they came from, or a survey of readers.

The first two can be checked with the data we have, but they only make the
story more or less plausible. Establishing it needs the third kind of evidence.

4. A comparison between two authors that collecting only the 500 most recent edits ruins.

Several answers work. A good one compares two authors over a fixed period of
time, since each author's 500 edits cover a different period.

Example: count the edits made to each page in 2023 and conclude that Chaucer's
article was more actively edited than Emily Brontë's that year. Brontë's page is
edited so often that her last 500 edits reach back only to late 2024, so her
count for 2023 comes out as zero. Chaucer's 500 edits reach back to 2021, so his
2023 count is a real number. The table shows Chaucer far ahead, when the true
result is probably the opposite.

The same problem affects any count of edits over a calendar period, such as
growth in article size over "the last five years" or edits per year. Comparing
the total number of edits is broken in a different way: every busy page has
exactly 500 because of the cap.

Nothing warns you. The data for missing years simply has no rows, which looks
the same as a year with no edits. A fair comparison either limits both authors
to the period both are observed (from the later of the two earliest
timestamps onward) or compares rates computed over each author's own window.

5. Why getting the right answer from .dt.convert_time_zone() on naive timestamps can be the problem.

Calling .dt.convert_time_zone() on a naive column assumes the times were in UTC.
Wikipedia does report revision timestamps in UTC, so on this data the assumption
holds and the output is correct.

The problem is that the correct output hides the assumption. Nothing in the code
says "these timestamps are UTC," so a reader cannot check whether the author
knew that or got lucky. Any check you run on this data passes, so there is
nothing to catch the mistake. When the same code is later reused on naive local
timestamps from another source, it shifts every one of them by the offset from
UTC, and it again gives no error or warning. Code that is right by accident is
harder to fix than code that fails, because it has already been trusted.

Writing .dt.replace_time_zone("UTC") first, then .dt.convert_time_zone(...),
states the assumption in the code, where a reader can see it and check it
against the source.
