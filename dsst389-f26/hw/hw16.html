Homework 16

[← back]

This reading covers four more ways to build a network out of the same corpus. Two of the questions ask you to build one by hand.

  1. Four outside pages link to the authors A, B, C, and D as follows: page 1 links to A, B, and C; page 2 links to A and B; page 3 links to B and C; page 4 links to C and D. Write the co-citation edge list, giving the count for every pair of authors that shares at least one citing page. Draw the network you get by keeping only edges with a count of 2 or more. Then suppose a fifth page is a list article that links to all four authors. Say what it does to your edge list, and use that to explain why the reading puts a threshold on the count.
  2. Five documents have these pairwise distances: A–B 0.20, A–C 0.50, A–D 0.90, A–E 1.00, B–C 0.30, B–D 0.80, B–E 0.95, C–D 0.40, C–E 0.85, D–E 0.35. Write each document's two nearest neighbors. Then build the symmetric nearest neighbor network with k = 2, keeping an edge only when two documents are mutually in each other's neighbor sets. Draw the result. Say which pairs were dropped for failing the mutual test, and what the mutual requirement prevents that a plain "link each document to its two closest" rule would not.
  3. In the directed network, Shakespeare has both the most links in and the most links out, while George Orwell has many links out and few in. The reading offers a temporal explanation for Orwell. State it. Then say why closeness centrality is unavailable once the network is directed, and what it would even mean to be close to a page you cannot reach.
  4. The page link network arranges authors by popularity, and the textual distance network from the previous chapter arranged them by era and genre. The two therefore disagree about who sits at the center. Say what each network is a picture of, and what you would conclude about an author who is central in one and peripheral in the other.
