Due at Project Workshop II, the last class before presentations. Your corpus should be collected by now, and this homework is where you find out whether it is the corpus you said it would be. Run the checks below and bring the code and its output to class, along with the files themselves. The class period is yours to work on the analysis with me in the room, so the more of this you have actually run, the more useful the two hours will be.

  1. Turn off your wifi and run your collection notebook from top to bottom. It should produce every table without reaching the network once. If it fails, your cache is not doing what you think it is doing, your raw layer is not a raw layer, and nothing else in this homework can be trusted until you fix it. Report what happened.
  2. Load every Parquet table. Print the schema of each and check the type of every column against the tables you described in Homework 44. Note every column whose type is not what you expected, and say what went wrong. Then print the row count of each table beside the count you predicted in the proposal, and explain any that are off by more than you can shrug at.
  3. The key checks. On the spine, compare .n_unique() against len() for both page_id and doc_id. Then, for every other table, anti-join it against the spine on page_id. All of these should come back empty. Report any that do not, find the offending rows, and explain how they got there.
  4. The resolution log. How many titles did you ask the API for, how many came back redirected, and how many resolved to no page at all? Print the redirects as a table of what you asked for and what you got, and look at them: some are your typos, some are Wikipedia disagreeing with your source about a name, and the two are not the same kind of problem. Name every title that resolved to nothing, and say for each whether it is a mistake in your list or a person, place, or thing that simply has no article.
  5. The coverage checks, which are the ones that decide whether you have a corpus or a pile of pages. Name the pages with no page view data. Print the ten shortest texts with their lengths, and say how many pages fall below the length at which text analysis stops meaning anything. Print the number of pages with no internal links to anywhere else in your corpus, since those are the pages that will sit alone at the edge of your network plot. Then answer the question plainly: is this the population you described in the proposal, or is it something else?
  6. The manifest. Print it. It must state the population rule, the retrieval date, the page view window, the revision cap, the row count of every table, and the source of each column you added by hand. This is what somebody else would have to read to know what you collected, and it is the document your datasheet grows out of.
  7. One rough plot for each research question. Among them, at least one built from the revisions or the page views, at least one from the text, and at least one from a network. Do not polish them. Bring them to class and we will talk about which of them are worth pursuing.
  8. Finally, a list of every problem the checks turned up. You can still collect more if you have to, but be honest about which of these are fixable by collecting and which are properties of the population you chose. For each one, say how you will handle it in the analysis, and write the sentence that will go in your datasheet admitting to it.
