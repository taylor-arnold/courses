This reading runs the 75 author pages through spaCy and turns them into a table with one row per token. The first three questions ask you to build a small piece of that table by hand, for the single sentence below. You will not get every cell right, and that is part of the point.

In 1847, Charlotte Brontë published Jane Eyre under the name Currer Bell.

  1. Write out the annotation table for this sentence with four columns, tid, token, lemma, and upos, numbering the tokens from one.
  2. Point out every row where the lemma differs from the token, and say what rule produced the difference.
  3. Add ent_type and ent_iob columns, marking each token B, I, or O and filling in an entity type wherever you expect the model to find one.
  4. Say what an author's list of top adjectives is actually a measurement of.
  5. Say why raw counts keep pushing generic words to the top no matter which part of speech you filter to.

Solutions

1. The annotation table for the sentence, with tid, token, lemma, and upos.

tid  token      lemma      upos
---  ---------  ---------  -----
  1  In         in         ADP
  2  1847       1847       NUM
  3  ,          ,          PUNCT
  4  Charlotte  Charlotte  PROPN
  5  Brontë     Brontë     PROPN
  6  published  publish    VERB
  7  Jane       Jane       PROPN
  8  Eyre       Eyre       PROPN
  9  under      under      ADP
 10  the        the        DET
 11  name       name       NOUN
 12  Currer     Currer     PROPN
 13  Bell       Bell       PROPN
 14  .          .          PUNCT

The comma and the final period are tokens of their own, so the sentence has 14
rows and not 12. Each name is split into one token per word. Getting the count
of rows right matters more than any single upos label; the model can also
make mistakes, such as tagging "Currer" as a common noun.

2. The rows where the lemma differs from the token, and the rule behind each.

There are two.

  - Row 1, "In" becomes "in". The capital letter is there only because the word
    starts the sentence, so the lemma removes it.
  - Row 6, "published" becomes "publish". Verbs are reduced to their base
    form, so the past tense ending is removed.

Every other row keeps its form. The proper nouns keep their capitals because
the capital is part of the name. "name" is already a singular noun, and the
number, punctuation, preposition, and article have no other form to reduce to.

3. Adding ent_type and ent_iob columns.

tid  token      ent_type      ent_iob
---  ---------  ------------  -------
  1  In                       O
  2  1847       DATE          B
  3  ,                        O
  4  Charlotte  PERSON        B
  5  Brontë     PERSON        I
  6  published                O
  7  Jane       WORK_OF_ART   B
  8  Eyre       WORK_OF_ART   I
  9  under                    O
 10  the                      O
 11  name                     O
 12  Currer     PERSON        B
 13  Bell       PERSON        I
 14  .                        O

Each entity starts with a B, its later tokens get an I, and everything outside
an entity is O with an empty ent_type. The sentence has four entities, and the
cumulative sum of the B rows from the reading would number them 1 to 4.

The doubtful row is Jane Eyre. The correct type is WORK_OF_ART because it is
a novel here, but it is also a woman's name, and the small model is much better
at people than at titles. It may well label those two tokens PERSON. That is
the error to expect, and it would make the PERSON count for this page include
a book. A good answer marks WORK_OF_ART as the right label and says the model
might not produce it. "Currer Bell" is a pen name, but the model sees two
capitalized words that look like a person's name, so PERSON is likely.

4. What an author's list of top adjectives measures.

It measures how the Wikipedia article about the author is written, after spaCy
has tagged and lemmatized it. The pages are about the authors and were written
by Wikipedia editors, so the list says nothing direct about the adjectives the
author used in their own books.

Two things are mixed in the list. Some adjectives describe the subject, such as
"metaphysical" for John Donne or "supernatural" for Ann Radcliffe, because
editors describe the author's work with them. Others, such as "more," "other,"
and "many," come from the editors' writing habits and appear on every page.
The counts also depend on the length of the article and on the tagger's
mistakes, since a word the model tags as something else never enters the list.

5. Why raw counts push generic words to the top whatever part of speech you filter to.

A raw count measures how often a word appears. Words that are common in all
English prose are also common on each page, so they win on any single page
too. Filtering by part of speech does not change that, because every part of
speech has its own common, general words. The nouns have "year," "life," and
"work," and the adjectives have "more," "other," and "many." Each filter just
brings a different set of generic words to the top.

What we usually want is the words that are frequent on this page compared with
the others, and a raw count has no information about the other pages. Answering
that needs a comparison with the rest of the collection. TF-IDF, in the next
chapter, weights each count down by how many documents the word appears in.
