This reading builds five things out of the annotation table: n-grams and collocations, named entities, grammatical patterns from the dependency parse, keyword-in-context displays, and complexity metrics. Two of the questions below ask you to compute by hand. Show your work; the arithmetic is the argument.

  1. Suppose a corpus has 1,000 alphabetic tokens and 999 bigrams, where "of" occurs 60 times, "the" 100 times, "of the" 20 times, "poet" 40 times, "laureate" 5 times, and "poet laureate" 5 times; compute the PMI of both bigrams, using natural logs as the reading does, and say which has the higher PMI.
  2. Explain why the code filters to bigrams occurring at least five times before ranking by PMI.
  3. For the token sequence below, first drop the tokens that fall outside any entity (the ones tagged O), as the reading does before this step; then write out the entity_id that a cumulative sum of ent_iob == "B" assigns to each remaining token, and finally the entity text reassembled from each group:

    Emily (PERSON, B), Brontë (PERSON, I), and (—, O), Charlotte (PERSON, B), Brontë (PERSON, I), lived (—, O), in (—, O), Haworth (GPE, B)

  4. Say what a claim like "Chaucer's page has the longest sentences" is really a claim about, given that Wikipedia editors wrote the pages.
  5. Say what a KWIC display of a word shows you that its raw count cannot.

Solutions

1. PMI of "of the" and "poet laureate" in a corpus of 1,000 tokens and 999 bigrams.

PMI(w1, w2) = log( P(w1, w2) / (P(w1) * P(w2)) )

The bigram probability divides by the 999 bigrams, and the word probabilities
divide by the 1,000 tokens, as in the reading's code.

"of the"
  P(of, the) = 20 / 999    = 0.02002
  P(of)      = 60 / 1000   = 0.06
  P(the)     = 100 / 1000  = 0.10
  P(of) * P(the)           = 0.006
  ratio = 0.02002 / 0.006  = 3.337
  PMI = log(3.337)         = 1.20

"poet laureate"
  P(poet, laureate) = 5 / 999  = 0.005005
  P(poet)           = 40 / 1000 = 0.04
  P(laureate)       = 5 / 1000  = 0.005
  P(poet) * P(laureate)         = 0.0002
  ratio = 0.005005 / 0.0002     = 25.03
  PMI = log(25.03)              = 3.22

"poet laureate" has the higher PMI, 3.22 against 1.20, although "of the"
occurs four times as often. "of the" appears about three times more often
than chance predicts, which is what you expect from two very common words.
"laureate" never appears except after "poet," so the pair appears 25 times
more often than chance predicts. That is the sign of a collocation.

2. Why the code keeps only bigrams that occur at least five times before ranking by PMI.

Because PMI is largest for pairs of rare words, and a pair seen once is mostly
noise. Take two words that each occur once in the corpus from question 1 and
happen to sit next to each other:

  P(w1, w2) = 1 / 999, P(w1) = P(w2) = 1 / 1000
  ratio = (1 / 999) / (1 / 1000)^2 = 1001
  PMI = log(1001) = 6.91

That is higher than "poet laureate" and it is the highest score any pair can
get, so without the filter the top of the ranking fills with one-off pairs:
typos, foreign phrases, odd names. A single co-occurrence cannot show that two
words belong together, since it may be chance. Requiring five occurrences
removes those pairs and leaves the ones where the pattern repeats. The number
five is a judgment call; a larger corpus could use a higher cutoff.

3. Entity ids and reassembled entity text for the token sequence.

After dropping the O tokens (and, lived, in), five tokens remain. The
new_entity flag is true on each B, and its cumulative sum gives the id:

token      ent_type  ent_iob  new_entity  entity_id
---------  --------  -------  ----------  ---------
Emily      PERSON    B        true        1
Brontë     PERSON    I        false       1
Charlotte  PERSON    B        true        2
Brontë     PERSON    I        false       2
Haworth    GPE       B        true        3

Grouping by entity_id and ent_type and joining the tokens with a space gives:

entity_id  ent_type  entity_text
---------  --------  ----------------
1          PERSON    Emily Brontë
2          PERSON    Charlotte Brontë
3          GPE       Haworth

The B on "Charlotte" is what keeps the two sisters apart. Once "and" is
dropped, their tokens are adjacent and share the type PERSON, so only the new
B starts a new id.

4. What "Chaucer's page has the longest sentences" is really a claim about.

It is a claim about the prose of the Wikipedia editors who wrote and revised
Chaucer's article. It says nothing about Chaucer's own sentences. The page is
written about Chaucer, in present-day encyclopedia English, by many people over
many years.

It is also a claim about how spaCy split the page into sentences, since the
metric averages words per sentence as the model counted them. Anything the
splitter handles badly, such as a quoted passage of Middle English verse or a
list with no periods, can turn into one long "sentence" and raise the average.
A careful statement would be "the text of Chaucer's Wikipedia article, as
split by spaCy, has the longest average sentence in the corpus." Before trusting
the ranking, it is worth reading the longest sentences on the page.

5. What a KWIC display of a word shows that its raw count cannot.

A count says how often a word appears. A KWIC display shows each use of the
word with the words around it, so you can see how it is used.

From the display you can tell which sense of the word is meant, what phrases it
sits in, whether it is usually a subject or an object, and which verbs and
adjectives go with it. You can also catch cases where the count is misleading:
a word that appears mostly inside a book title or a quotation, a phrase that is
repeated as boilerplate across many pages, or two different words that were
given the same lemma. A count of "poetry" treats all of these the same.

For this reason the reading recommends a quick concordance whenever a
frequency table suggests that a word matters:

DSText.kwic(anno, "poetry", max_num=15, window=5)
