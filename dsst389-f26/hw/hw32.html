The reading loads state from a GeoJSON file. It has fifty rows, one per state, and only three columns of attributes: name, abb, and fips. Everything else in the table is geometry. On a sheet of paper, hand write answers to the following.

  1. Explain why the distortion from projection is easier to see in the state polygons than in the metro points from the last reading.
  2. Say what is actually wrong with a centroid or an area computed in raw longitude and latitude.
  3. Name a state whose centroid falls outside its own borders, and say what that should make you cautious about when centroids stand in for states.
  4. Name one variable you would want to join onto state, and say whether it should be a count or a rate and why.
  5. Say why a file format would store a single-part shape like Colorado as a MultiPolygon anyway.

Solutions

1. Why projection distortion is easier to see in the state polygons than in the metro points.

A point has no shape and no size. Distortion moves it, but a dot in the wrong
spot still looks exactly like a dot, and you have nothing to check it against.
The whole cloud of metro areas comes out roughly country-shaped under any of
these projections, so the eye has no way to catch the error.

A polygon carries a shape you already know. Florida has a panhandle, Texas has
its notch, Maine leans the way it leans, and you have seen all of them since
grade school. Stretching shows up immediately against that memory.

Polygons also let you compare sizes within one picture. The east-west stretch
grows as you go north, so in unprojected coordinates Montana and North Dakota
get visibly inflated against Texas and Florida. Two states you know the
relative sizes of, drawn wrong side by side, is a much louder signal than a
point that has quietly slid fifty kilometers.

2. What actually goes wrong in a centroid or an area computed on raw longitude and latitude.

For area, the result is in squared degrees, and that is not a unit of area. You
cannot fix it by multiplying through by a constant, because a degree of
longitude is not a fixed length: about 111 km at the equator, about 85 km at 40
degrees north. Every state's east-west extent is counted at its full equatorial
worth, so northern states are inflated more than southern ones. The damage is
not that the numbers are in the wrong unit, it is that the RANKING changes.
Sorting by squared degrees is not the same list as sorting by square
kilometers.

For the centroid, the problem is the same stretch seen from the other side. The
centroid is the balance point of the shape in whatever plane you compute it in,
and raw longitude and latitude give you a plane that is stretched east-west,
increasingly so toward the south. The top of a state gets more weight than it
has earned, and the centroid drifts north of the true center. It is still a
point on the map, it is still inside the state most of the time, and nothing
warns you that it is the wrong point.

Both are fixed the same way, by projecting first:

state = (
    funs.read_file("data/acs_state.geojson")
    .geo.to_crs(5069)
)

3. A state whose centroid falls outside its own borders, and what that should make you cautious about.

Hawaii is the clearest case: the state is a chain of islands, and the balance
point of the chain sits in open ocean. Michigan is the other standard example,
with its two peninsulas putting the centroid out in Lake Michigan. Alaska is
worth a mention too, since the Aleutians drag its centroid a long way west of
anywhere anyone lives.

The immediate caution is mechanical. If you replace each state by its centroid
and then join those points back against state polygons, Hawaii and Michigan
match nothing, and a state's own point can land inside a neighbor. Distances
"from Michigan" measured off that point start in the water.

The larger caution is that the centroid is a summary, and it drops everything
about extent. It is not the population center, it is not the capital, and it is
not where anything happens; it is a fact about the outline. Any question whose
answer depends on how far a state SPREADS, rather than roughly where it sits,
is a question the centroid cannot be trusted with.

4. One variable to join onto state, and whether it should be a count or a rate.

There is no single right variable here; what the answer has to do is commit to
one and give the reason. One good choice is health insurance coverage, joined
on by the state name or the FIPS code.

It should be a RATE: the share of residents without coverage, not the number of
them. On a choropleth the fill covers the whole polygon, so the reader takes it
as a property of the whole state, and every count map ends up as a population
map with California and Texas on top. Worse, the states that dominate the
picture visually are the big empty western ones, so a count map manages to
mislead in two directions at once.

The reading's rule is the short version: choropleths want variables that are
already area-based or per-person, like density or a percentage. Counts are
sometimes the quantity you genuinely care about, but then a map is the wrong
display for them and a sorted bar chart is the right one.

5. Why a format would store a single-part shape like Colorado as a MultiPolygon.

Because the column has to hold every state, and some states are genuinely
multi-part. Hawaii is islands, Michigan has two peninsulas plus islands, Alaska
has the Aleutians, Massachusetts has Nantucket, Virginia has its Eastern Shore.
Many formats declare one geometry type for a whole layer, and a Shapefile
requires it outright, so the type has to be the one that can hold the hardest
row. Wrapping Colorado in a MultiPolygon with a single member costs nothing:
the shape it describes is identical, and the file gains a column where every
row has the same type.

The payoff is that nothing downstream has to branch. Code that walks the parts
of a geometry works on all fifty states without a special case for the simple
ones, and operations that can split a shape (clipping to a coastline,
intersecting with a buffer) can turn a single-part state into a multi-part one
without changing the type of the column. It is easier to always use the general
type than to switch to it the first time you need it.
