Homework 44

[← back]

Assigned October 14th and due at Project Workshop I on October 28th. This is the proposal for the final project, and it is the last thing you write before you start collecting. Read the project description before you start. In class you will hand this to a partner whose job is to break your population: to name a page your rule lets in that does not belong, and one it keeps out that does. The more of it you have written down, the more useful that will be. Populations are locked at the end of class and collection begins that afternoon.

You have two weeks, and you are given two weeks because the choice of population is the decision this project cannot recover from. Nothing below depends on Exam II, so none of it needs to wait for the exam and none of it should be written the night after it. Pick a candidate population in the first week, look at its pages, and be willing to throw it away and pick another. Unlike the other homeworks, type your answers rather than hand writing them, so that they can be submitted through Box as part of your final project. Bring a copy to class as well, since you will work from it in the workshop.

  1. The population, written as a rule. One or two sentences, precise enough that somebody else could follow them and arrive at the same set of pages: which language edition, which categories or which source list or which Wikidata criteria, whether you descend into subcategories, and what you do with pages that are lists, disambiguations, or redirects. Then say how many pages you expect the rule to produce, and how you know.
  2. The edges of the rule. Name one page that your rule admits and that you think does not belong in the population, and one page that it excludes and that you think does. For each, say whether you are going to change the rule or keep it and document the problem. Both are defensible answers; pretending the problem is not there is not.
  3. Three or four research questions. Each one should be a question you genuinely do not know the answer to, and each one should be answerable by comparing things rather than by counting them. Under each question, write the tables it needs, and write the one sentence you would have to be able to say at the end of the project in order to have answered it.
  4. The metadata you will add yourself. At least two columns per page that do not come from the API. For each: its name, its type, its units or its expected set of values, the source you will take it from, named specifically enough that a reader could check it, and how you will code the cases where the source is ambiguous or silent. Then say which research question each column serves. If a column serves none of them, cut it.
  5. The tables. List every table you plan to build. For each one give its name, its unit of observation, its primary key, every foreign key along with the table it points to, and the number of rows you expect it to have. Two of these are decisions rather than facts, and you must state them explicitly: the cap on revisions per page, and the fixed window for page views. Give both, with a sentence of justification for each.
  6. Three pages by hand. Pick three pages from your population, one you expect to be long and prominent, one you expect to be short and obscure, and one in between. Open each in a browser and record: roughly how long the article is, how many revisions its history holds and when the oldest one you can see was made, and how many of the links in its opening section point at other pages in your own population. Then say what those three pages lead you to expect about the other hundred, and name the one thing you saw that worries you most.
  7. The two parts of your proposal you are least sure about, written out as questions for your partner. These are what you will trade in class.
