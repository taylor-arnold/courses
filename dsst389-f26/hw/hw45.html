The first three questions use the small undirected network given by the edge list below. Draw it on your paper first, then compute the metrics by hand. Put the nodes wherever they look clearest; the drawing is a tool, not an answer.

A–B, A–C, B–C, C–D, D–E, E–F, E–G, F–G

  1. Draw the network, write the degree of every node, and say how many components it has.
  2. Compute the closeness centrality of A and of D from their shortest-path distances to every other node.
  3. Find the node that lies on every shortest path between the two triangles, and explain how betweenness can rank it first while its degree is among the lowest in the network.
  4. Split your drawing into two clusters and say which cluster node D belongs in and why the answer is not obvious.
  5. Say what an edge in the Wikipedia network actually records, given that no author in the corpus ever linked to anyone.

Solutions

1. Draw the network, give each node's degree, and count the components.

One clear drawing puts a triangle on each side with D in the middle:

    A                   F
    | \                / |
    |   C --- D --- E    |
    | /                \ |
    B                   G

Degrees (the number of edges touching each node):

  A  2      B  2      C  3      D  2      E  3      F  2      G  2

The degrees add up to 16, twice the 8 edges, which is a quick check. There is
one component: every node can be reached from every other by following edges,
so the network is connected.

2. Closeness centrality of A and of D.

Shortest-path distances (number of edges):

  from A:  B 1, C 1, D 2, E 3, F 4, G 4      total 15
  from D:  C 1, E 1, A 2, B 2, F 2, G 2      total 10

Closeness is the reciprocal of the total distance:

  A:  1 / 15 = 0.067
  D:  1 / 10 = 0.100

Many programs multiply by the number of other nodes (6), which gives
6 / 15 = 0.40 for A and 6 / 10 = 0.60 for D. The reading's close column uses
the harmonic version, which sums the reciprocals of the distances instead:

  A:  1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3.33
  D:  1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 4.00

Every version ranks D above A. D is at most two steps from anything, while A
is four steps from F and G.

3. The node on every shortest path between the two triangles, and why its betweenness is highest while its degree is among the lowest.

D. The only route from the left triangle {A, B, C} to the right one {E, F, G}
is C - D - E, so every shortest path between the two sides passes through D.

Betweenness counts, for each pair of other nodes, whether the shortest path
between them runs through the node. Each pair here has only one shortest path:

  D:  every pair with one node on each side, 3 * 3 = 9 pairs
  C:  A or B paired with D, E, F, or G,     2 * 4 = 8 pairs
  E:  F or G paired with A, B, C, or D,     2 * 4 = 8 pairs
  A, B, F, G:  0, since no shortest path runs through them

So D ranks first with 9, even though its degree of 2 ties for the lowest in
the network. Degree counts neighbors, and betweenness counts the routes that
depend on a node. D has only two neighbors, but it is the only bridge between
the two halves. Removing D would split the network into two groups of three.
Removing C or E would also disconnect it, but would cut off only two nodes.

4. Split the network into two clusters and decide where D belongs.

The two clusters are the triangles, {A, B, C} and {E, F, G}, since each has
three edges inside it and one edge leading out.

D can go in either one, and neither is better. D has one edge to each side
(C - D and D - E), and the network is a mirror image around D. Putting D with
{A, B, C} leaves one edge between the clusters (D - E). Putting it with
{E, F, G} also leaves one (C - D). A clustering method that tries to keep
edges inside groups has no reason to prefer either, so the result depends on
how the algorithm breaks ties, such as the order of the nodes in the edge list.

Either answer is correct if it gives this reason. The general point is that
bridge nodes are the ones whose cluster labels are least reliable. When a
clustering on real data puts a node like this in one group, the choice tells
you little about that node.

5. What an edge in the Wikipedia network records.

It records that a Wikipedia editor put a link from one author's article to
another author's article, in the main body text, and that the link was still
there when the data was collected. The authors themselves never linked to
anything. The edge is a record of how the encyclopedia is written.

Several things shape whether an edge exists. The article has to mention the
other author, and Wikipedia's convention is to link the first mention of a
subject that has its own article. Longer articles mention more people, so
they have more outgoing links. The link could have been added or removed at
any point, so the network describes one snapshot of the pages. In the
undirected version, the direction of the link has also been thrown away.

So an edge is evidence that editors thought the second author worth mentioning
in the first author's article. That often follows a real connection, such as
influence, friendship, a biography, or a rivalry, but the edge does not say
which one, and a missing edge does not show that no connection existed.
