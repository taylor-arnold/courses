This reading finishes the chapter with four operations that change the geometries themselves: buffering, dissolving, exploding, and simplifying. On a sheet of paper, hand write answers to the following.

  1. Say what it means for a piece of ground to fall inside two 100 kilometer discs, and why that breaks adding up the population the discs cover.
  2. Say which part of dissolve corresponds to the grouping and which part corresponds to the aggregation.
  3. Explain why summing is the right aggregation for area but the wrong one for the median household income column.
  4. After explode copies the state area onto every Hawaiian island, say which columns of the exploded table are now false and what you must recompute to fix them.
  5. Explain why the reading tells you to simplify geometries for display rather than for analysis.

Solutions

1. What it means for ground to sit inside two 100 kilometer discs, and why that breaks a population total.

The discs overlap. Two metro centers less than 200 kilometers apart have buffers
that cross, and every point in the crossing is within 100 kilometers of both
centers. Baltimore and Washington are the easy example: the land between them
belongs to both discs at once.

That is fine as a description of the ground, and it is fatal if you add the
discs up. Summing a population figure over the buffers counts the people in the
overlap once for each disc that covers them, so the total comes out larger than
the number of people who actually live in the covered region. The more crowded
the map, the worse it gets, and the eastern seaboard is where the buffers pile
into a nearly solid band.

The fix is to stop treating the discs as separate pieces. Dissolve them into one
geometry first, so the shared ground is part of a single region, and then
measure the population of that region.

2. Which part of dissolve is the grouping and which part is the aggregation.

The by argument is the grouping. It names the column whose values define the
groups, exactly the way group_by does, so by="side" puts every state with side
equal to "West" in one group and the rest in the other.

The aggfunc argument is the aggregation, and it applies to the ordinary
non-geometry columns. Writing aggfunc={"area": "sum"} says that the area of a
group is the sum of the areas of its states.

The geometries have an aggregation too, and it is the one part you do not get to
choose: the group's geometries are merged into a single geometry with the
internal borders erased. That merge is the whole point of dissolve, and it is
what group_by and agg cannot do on their own.

3. Why summing works for area but not for median household income.

Area is additive. Two states that do not overlap have a combined area equal to
the sum of the two areas, so summing the column gives the right answer for the
merged region, and it matches what the merged geometry would report if you
recomputed its area directly.

Median household income is not additive. Adding Virginia's median to Maryland's
produces a number in the neighborhood of $180,000, which is not the median
income of anywhere, and does not describe any household in either state.
Medians do not add, and a value already computed as a median cannot be combined
into a larger median at all, since you would need the underlying household
records to do that.

The closest honest substitute is a population weighted mean of the state
medians, which is an approximation of the regional median rather than the real
thing. The general rule: sums are safe for extensive quantities like area, land
count, and population, and wrong for intensive ones like rates, densities,
medians, and per capita figures.

4. Which columns of the exploded Hawaii table are now false, and what to recompute.

Every column that describes the state as a whole, because explode copies the
state's values onto each island without changing them. The area column is the
clear one: after the explode, each of the Hawaiian rows claims the area of the
entire state, so the eight of them together report roughly eight times too much
land. Anything else derived from the state's geometry is wrong for the same
reason, including the centroid coordinate x, the side column built from it, and
a vertex count from count_coordinates. A population column would be false in the
same way, though the value would at least be recoverable if you had per island
figures.

The name column is fine, and so is the geometry column. Those are the two you
can trust, and the geometry is what lets you rebuild the rest.

(
    state
    .geo.explode()
    .filter(c.name == "Hawaii")
    .geo.area
)

The .geo.area call overwrites the copied column with each island's own area. Anything else that was computed from the state polygon has to be recomputed the same way, after the explode rather than before it.

5. Why the reading says to simplify for display and not for analysis.

Because simplify works on one geometry at a time. It drops vertices from each
polygon independently, keeping that polygon within the tolerance you gave it,
and it has no idea that the vertex it just removed from Kansas was also on
Nebraska's border. The two boundaries were identical and now they are not, so
small gaps and slivers of overlap open up along shared edges.

For a plot that costs nothing. The gaps are far smaller than the width of a
drawn line, the map looks the same, and it draws faster because Alaska is no
longer carrying tens of thousands of vertices you cannot see.

For analysis it matters, because the predicates are exact. touches asks whether
two polygons share a boundary, and after simplifying they may not share one
anymore, so a pair of neighboring states can quietly stop being neighbors.
Areas shift, an intersects join near a border can go either way, and a point
close to a coastline can land on the wrong side of it. Do the analysis on the
full geometries and save the simplification for the last step, when you are
drawing.
