The reading joins the two tables we already have: metro, with one point per metropolitan area, and state, with one polygon per state, both projected into EPSG:5069. On a sheet of paper, hand write answers to the following. A few sentences apiece is enough.

  1. An ordinary join matches rows whose key values are equal. Say what takes the place of the key in metro.geo.sjoin(state). The reading then runs the join in the other order, state.geo.sjoin(metro), and gets a different table. Explain what differs between the two results and give a question you would answer with each one.
  2. A metro area that straddles a state line is assigned to whichever state contains its center point. Kansas City is in the data, and it is a metro that spans Missouri and Kansas. Suppose you used the join to compute the total metro population of every state. Say what that number gets wrong for Missouri and Kansas, whether the error would be visible anywhere in the output, and how you would find out that it happened.
  3. The self-join with predicate="touches" returns each shared border twice, once from each side. Explain why, in terms of what the join is matching. Then say which predicate you would use to find every state that a 100 kilometer disc around a metro center reaches into, and why within would be the wrong choice there.
  4. metro.geo.sjoin_nearest(metro, exclusive=True, distance_col="distance") gives every metro area the distance to its closest neighbor, in meters. Say what exclusive=True is protecting you from. Then describe the shape you would expect a histogram of that distance column to have, say how the distances would differ between metros east and west of the Mississippi, and name something about how the country is settled that this one column reveals.
