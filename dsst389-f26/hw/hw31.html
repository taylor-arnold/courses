The reading uses metro, a dataset with one row for each metropolitan statistical area in the United States and columns for population, economic indicators, and the coordinates lon and lat. On a sheet of paper, hand write answers to the following and bring them to class.

  1. State the two objections the reading raises to plotting raw lat against lon, and say which one is about accuracy and which is about use.
  2. Explain what the number 4326 records, and why the string POINT (-86.8 32.4) on its own does not identify a place on Earth.
  3. Give one analysis of metro that comes out wrong if you work in EPSG:4326, and say whether its distances come out too large or too small.
  4. Explain in your own words why Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) makes Greenland look bigger than Africa.
  5. Name one question about metro areas that the single-point simplification can still answer honestly, and one for which it would quietly mislead you.

Solutions

1. The two objections to plotting lat against lon directly, and which is about accuracy and which is about use.

The accuracy objection: the plot treats longitude and latitude as ordinary
Cartesian coordinates, when they describe positions on a curved surface. Equal
steps in longitude cover less ground the further north you go, so the top of
the plot is stretched east-west relative to the bottom. The picture is not just
ugly, it is wrong about the relative positions of the cities in it.

The use objection: there is nothing on the plot to orient by. No coastlines, no
state borders, nothing but dots on a blank field. A reader who does not already
know the shape of the country cannot tell what they are looking at.

2. What the number 4326 records, and why a WKT string on its own does not identify a place.

4326 is an EPSG code, and it records the coordinate reference system that the
numbers belong to: WGS 84, the latitude/longitude system GPS uses, with both
coordinates measured in degrees. It is what `points_from_xy` writes into the
`_crs` column, and it is the default because most raw coordinate data arrives
that way.

POINT (-86.8 32.4) on its own is a pair of numbers with no statement of what
they are coordinates *in*. Read as WGS 84 they land in Alabama; read as meters
in a projected system they land somewhere else entirely, or off the map. The
geometry supplies the coordinates and the CRS supplies the rule for turning
them into a place. You need both halves, which is why the two columns travel
together.

3. An analysis of metro that comes out wrong in EPSG:4326, and the direction of the error.

Several answers work here, since any distance, area, or buffer computed on
unprojected coordinates is suspect. A good one is the nearest-neighbor join:

(
    metro
    .geo.sjoin_nearest(metro, exclusive=True, distance_col="distance")
)

In EPSG:4326 the distances come back in degrees, and a degree is not a fixed
length. One degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator but only about
85 km at 40 degrees north, roughly the latitude of New York. The calculation
counts every east-west degree at its full equatorial worth, so the distances
come out TOO LARGE, and the error grows the further north the pair sits.

The damage is not that the numbers are in the wrong unit. It is that an
east-west neighbor is penalized against a north-south one, so the join can hand
back the wrong nearest city, and nothing raises an error when it does.

4. Why Web Mercator makes Greenland look bigger than Africa.

Mercator is built to preserve shape. Lines of longitude converge toward the
poles on the globe but are drawn parallel on the map, so the map has to stretch
east-west more and more as you go north. To keep shapes from being squashed, it
then stretches north-south by that same factor. Stretching both directions
equally is what keeps angles and shapes right, but it multiplies area by the
SQUARE of the factor, and the factor grows without bound toward the poles.

Africa straddles the equator, where the factor is about 1 and the drawing is
close to true size. Greenland sits between roughly 60 and 83 degrees north,
where the factor is large, so it is inflated many times over. Africa is about
14 times the area of Greenland, and on the map the two look about the same.

This is the trade-off from the reading in its plainest form: you can preserve
shape or area, but not both, and Web Mercator chose shape.

5. One question the single-point simplification answers honestly, and one where it misleads.

There is no single right pair here. What matters is the distinction: the honest
question only needs to know roughly WHERE the metro area is, and the misleading
one needs to know how far it SPREADS.

Answered honestly: which state is each metro area in, or how many metro areas
lie west of the Mississippi. A metro area is small next to a state, so its
center point falls in the right one nearly every time, and the answer does not
depend on the metro having any size at all.

Quietly misleading: does this metro area cross a state line, or how many of its
residents live within 20 km of the coast. A point can only be in one state, so
the join reports a single state for Kansas City exactly as it does for Boise,
and never signals that one of them straddles a border. The second question is
worse, because the whole population sits at one coordinate: a buffer either
catches all of it or none of it, when the true answer is some fraction in
between. The output looks the same in both cases, which is what makes it quiet.
