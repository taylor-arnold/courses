Exam I Study Guide

[← back] [reference sheet]

The first exam is 75 minutes. It has six tasks, labeled A through F, with fifteen questions spread across them. Every task gives you a small dataset — a printed table, a map drawn to scale, or both — together with the code that runs on it. Your job is to say what comes out. This is the reverse of what you did in DSST289: there you were given a result and asked to write the code, and here you are given the code and the data and asked for the result.

You do not need to memorize any function names or arguments. The reference sheet will be attached to your exam. It lists every method you could possibly need, with its arguments and defaults. This is the point: the code in this course is specific to a domain and is not worth committing to memory, while knowing which operation a question calls for, and what it does to your data, is the entire skill. The exam tests the second thing and hands you the first. Bring a calculator and a pencil; you will be drawing on the map.

The exam covers Chapter 11 and the material in Homeworks 1 through 4. Questions come in five forms:

  • Fill in the output. A small table and a code block; write the table that comes back, or a named column of it, or just the number of rows.
  • Compute by hand. An area, a distance, a centroid, a row count. Exact numbers, with units.
  • Find the trap. Code that almost answers the question asked, containing one of the errors we have discussed all unit. Circle the offending line and say what the output shows instead.
  • Choose the code. Several candidate blocks, one of which produces the stated result.
  • One sentence, with an anchor. A short interpretation, always paired with something checkable: name the row that proves it, or the number that would change.

What You Need To Be Able To Do

Only the following will be needed for the exam.

Geometries and coordinate systems.

  1. Given a table with lon and lat columns, name the two columns that points_from_xy adds and write out their exact contents for a given row.
  2. Given a geometry string, say whether its coordinates could be longitude and latitude or must come from a projected system, and say what the numbers are measured in either way.
  3. Explaining why a WKT string on its own does not identify a place on Earth, and what the _crs column supplies.
  4. Given a described analysis, saying whether EPSG:4326 is adequate or whether the data must be projected first, and why. You are never asked to recall an EPSG code; the codes and their purposes are on the reference sheet.
  5. Saying what unit an area or a distance comes back in for a given CRS, and converting between square meters and square kilometers.

Polygons and metrics.

  1. Given a map drawn to scale, computing the area of a polygon in both square meters and square kilometers.
  2. Given a map, computing a centroid, and identifying a geometry whose centroid falls outside of itself.
  3. Saying which of centroid, area, buffer, and x replace the geometry column and which append a new column of ordinary numbers.
  4. Given a choropleth of a count, saying why it misleads, and writing the line that turns the count into a rate.

Spatial joins.

  1. Given a map of points and polygons, filling in the output of sjoin: the number of rows, which rows are matched to which, and which table's geometry survives.
  2. Saying what changes when the two tables are swapped, and which of the two orders a given question calls for.
  3. Identifying the rows that a spatial join silently drops, and saying how you would find out that it happened.
  4. Given a map, listing every pair of polygons satisfying a named predicate (touches, intersects, within, contains), and counting the rows of a self-join.
  5. Given a buffer distance, saying which polygons a buffered point reaches, and saying what the distance is measured in.
  6. Given sjoin_nearest, computing the nearest neighbor and the distance for a named row, and saying what exclusive=True protects you from.

Changing the geometries.

  1. Given a grouping column, filling in the output of dissolve: the number of rows, the aggregated columns, and whether each merged geometry is a Polygon or a MultiPolygon.
  2. Saying which aggregation functions are legitimate for a given column and which produce a number that is quietly wrong.
  3. Given explode, saying how many rows come back, naming the columns that are now false, and saying what recomputes them.
  4. Saying why simplify is for display rather than analysis, and naming the join it can break.

We will not have reading GeoJSON by hand, writing WKT for anything more complicated than a point, the internals of R-trees, the crosses and overlaps predicates, explore beyond knowing what it is for, or any EPSG code that is not on the reference sheet.

Practice Exam Questions and Answers

The questions below use two tables. metro has one row for each metro area, with the columns name, pop (population, in thousands), and geometry. state has one row for each state, with the columns name, abb, side, and geometry. Both are in EPSG:5069, whose unit is the meter. The map is drawn to scale; its axes are labeled in kilometers for legibility.

The pop column of metro is as follows. The side column of state is "West" for Ash, Birch, and Cedar, and "East" for Dune and Elm.

namepopnamepopnamepop
Alder900Juno2400Lark1100
Hollis1500Kester600Marsh800

Task A. Building Geometries

metro = pl.read_csv("data/metro.csv")
metro = metro.geo.points_from_xy("lon", "lat")

1. Name the two columns that the second line adds, and give the exact contents of each for a metro area whose lon is -77.4 and whose lat is 37.5.

A column `geometry` holding POINT (-77.4 37.5), and a column `_crs` holding
4326. The first records where the point is; the second records what system
those two numbers are expressed in.

2. Three of the four geometry strings below cannot be in EPSG:4326. Circle the one that can, and say in one sentence what the other three are most likely measured in.

POINT (1573000 1892000)     POINT (-2100000 550000)
POINT (-77.4 37.5)          POINT (312450 4187600)

POINT (-77.4 37.5). Longitude runs from -180 to 180 and latitude from -90 to
90, so the other three, whose coordinates run into the hundreds of thousands
and the millions, must be projected coordinates measured in meters.

3. Explain in one sentence why POINT (-86.8 32.4), on its own, does not identify a place on Earth.

Because two numbers are not a location until you say what they are coordinates
*in*. The same pair names a spot in Alabama under EPSG:4326 and a completely
different spot, or no spot at all, under a projected system. The `_crs` column
supplies the missing half of the statement.

Task B. Area

state = funs.read_file("data/state.geojson")
state = state.geo.area
state.select(c.name, c.area)

4. This code does not do what it appears to do. Circle the line that causes the problem, say what the values in the area column are measured in, and write the one line that fixes it.

The offending line is `state = state.geo.area`, but only because of what is
missing before it: the table is still in EPSG:4326 as it came off the disk, so
the areas come back in SQUARED DEGREES, a unit that means nothing and that
varies with latitude. The values are not merely in the wrong unit; they are not
even proportional to the true areas. The fix is to project first:

state = state.geo.to_crs(5069)

Nothing raises an error, which is the point. You get a column of
plausible-looking numbers and no warning at all.

5. After your fix, fill in the area column for all five states, in square kilometers. Then say what number the area column actually holds for Cedar, given that the code returns the CRS unit rather than the one you just used.

Ash 40,000; Birch 40,000; Cedar 80,000; Dune 15,000; Elm 15,000 square
kilometers. The `area` column itself is in square meters, so Cedar's value is
8.0e10. To get square kilometers you divide by 1,000,000.

6. You want a choropleth of population density rather than of total population. Write the single with_columns line that creates the density column, being careful about units, and say in one sentence which state the total-population map would have made look most important for the wrong reason.

state.with_columns(dens = c.pop / (c.area / 1_000_000))

Cedar, which is twice the area of any other state and would dominate the page
under any color scale, while holding only Kester's 600.

Task C. Spatial Joins

(
    metro
    .geo.sjoin(state)
    .group_by(c.name_right)
    .agg(n = pl.len(), pop = c.pop.sum())
)

7. Fill in the output table. The rows may come back in any order.

name_right    n     pop
Ash           2    2400
Birch         1    2400
Cedar         1     600
Dune          1    1100

8. The pop column of metro sums to 7300, but the pop column of this output sums to 6500. Say where the missing 800 went, name the metro area responsible, and say what property of sjoin let it disappear without an error.

It went to Marsh, at (420, 350), which falls in the gap between Cedar and Elm
and is therefore inside no state at all. `sjoin` is an INNER join by default: a
left row with no match is dropped, silently, and the only place the loss shows
up is in a total that no longer adds up. This is why you check the row count of
a spatial join against the row count you went in with.

9. Exactly one state is absent from the output. Name it, say why it is absent, and say in one sentence why a reader of this table alone cannot tell the difference between a state with no metro areas and a state that was dropped for some other reason.

Elm, because it contains no metro areas, so it never matched anything and the
inner join dropped it. From this table alone you cannot tell that apart from
Elm having been lost to a CRS mismatch, a broken geometry, or a typo in a name,
because in every one of those cases Elm is simply not there. An absent row and
a zero look identical, and only a zero is a fact about the world.

10. Now suppose the first two lines were replaced with state.geo.sjoin(metro). How many rows does that call return, what kind of geometry is in the output, and which single row of metro is missing from it?

Five rows. The geometry is the POLYGONS, because `sjoin` keeps the geometry of
the left table and the states are now on the left. Marsh is still missing, for
the same reason as before, but Elm is now present, matched against nothing.
Note that both orders return five rows here, and they are not the same five:
swapping the tables changes which side gets dropped.

Task D. Predicates and Buffers

state.geo.sjoin(state, predicate="touches")

11. How many rows does this return? List the pairs. Then name the two states that do not appear in the output at all, and say what that tells you about them.

Six rows: (Ash, Birch), (Birch, Ash), (Ash, Cedar), (Cedar, Ash), (Birch,
Cedar), (Cedar, Birch). Three shared borders, each appearing twice, because the
join matches every state against every other state and a border seen from Ash
is the same border seen from Birch.

Dune and Elm appear nowhere: they touch nothing, neither each other (the gap
from y = 100 to y = 200 separates them) nor the western block.

(
    metro
    .filter(c.name == "Juno")
    .geo.buffer(100_000)
    .geo.sjoin(state)
)

12. How many rows does this return, and which states are they? Draw the buffer on the map. Then say why the argument is 100_000 rather than 100, and what the geometry column of the output holds.

Three rows: Ash, Birch, and Cedar. Juno sits at (280, 120), which is 80 km from
Ash's eastern edge at x = 200 and 80 km from Cedar's southern edge at y = 200,
so a 100 km disc reaches into both, on top of the Birch that contains it.
Dune's nearest edge is 170 km away and does not make it.

The argument is 100_000 because the buffer is measured in the units of the
current CRS, and EPSG:5069 is in meters. The geometry column holds the DISC,
not the point: `buffer` replaces the geometry rather than appending a column.

13. Suppose you ran the same buffer on the data before projecting it, while it was still in EPSG:4326. Say what 100_000 would then be asking for, and what the subsequent sjoin would return.

A disc 100,000 DEGREES in radius, which is some 250 times the circumference of
the Earth. The buffer would swallow every state, so the sjoin would return all
five, and the answer would look like a finding rather than a mistake.

Task E. Nearest Neighbors

metro.geo.sjoin_nearest(metro, exclusive=True, distance_col="distance")

14. How many rows does this return? Give the nearest neighbor and the exact value of distance for Alder and for Kester.

Six rows, one for each row of the left table.

Alder's nearest neighbor is Hollis, at distance = 100,000. (A 60 km and 80 km
offset, so 100 km, and the column is in meters.)

Kester's is Marsh, at distance = 130,000. (120 km and 50 km, so 130 km.)

Note that Marsh, which the containment join threw away, is the nearest neighbor
of a metro that the join kept: being in no state does not make it absent from
the data.

15. Say what exclusive=True is protecting you from, and give the exact distance column that would come back without it.

It stops each metro area from matching itself. Since `metro` is being joined to
`metro`, every row is its own closest feature at a distance of exactly zero, so
without it the distance column comes back as six zeros and every row is matched
to itself.

Task F. Dissolve and Explode

region = state.geo.dissolve(by="side", aggfunc={"area": "sum"})

16. Fill in the output, giving the area column in square kilometers. Then say which of the two merged geometries is a Polygon and which is a MultiPolygon, and describe the exact shape of the West geometry.

side     area
West   160,000
East    30,000

West is a POLYGON: Ash, Birch, and Cedar are contiguous, and dissolving them
erases the internal borders to leave a single 400 km by 400 km square, whose
area, 160,000 km^2, is exactly the sum of its parts.

East is a MULTIPOLYGON: Dune and Elm do not touch, so there is no way to merge
them into one connected shape, and the geometry keeps them as two separate
pieces under a single row.

17. Calling region.geo.centroid puts the center of the East region at POINT (525000 150000). Mark it on the map. Say what is remarkable about where it falls, and say in one sentence what that should make you cautious about if you were using centroids to stand in for regions.

It falls at (525, 150), in the empty gap between Dune and Elm, outside the East
region entirely. Since the two parts have equal areas, the centroid is just the
midpoint of their two centers, and the midpoint of a shape with a hole in the
middle need not lie in the shape.

A centroid is a convenient single point to stand in for a region, but it is not
guaranteed to be a point OF that region, so any analysis that treats it as one
(what is the nearest hospital to this region? which county is this region in?)
can return an answer about a place the region does not occupy.

18. Calling region.geo.explode() on the result gives back a table with how many rows? Write out the area column of that table, say which of its values are now false, and give the single call that repairs them.

Three rows: West is a single Polygon and stays one row, while East is a
MultiPolygon of two parts and becomes two. The area column comes back as
160,000, 30,000, and 30,000.

The first is still true. The two East values are now FALSE, because `explode`
copies the region-level column onto every part, and neither part is 30,000
km^2: each is 15,000. Chaining `.geo.area` recomputes the column from the
geometry each row actually has.

region.geo.explode().geo.area

19. Summing is the right aggregation for area. Say in one sentence why the same aggfunc would produce a wrong number for a median household income column, even though both columns are just numbers.

Because area is EXTENSIVE and income is not. Two states put together really do
have the sum of their areas, but they do not have the sum of their median
incomes, and the sum of two medians is not a quantity that means anything at
all. The aggregation would still run and still produce a number, which is
exactly the danger.
