Final Project: A Corpus of Your Own

[← back]

Since September we have been living inside a corpus somebody else built. Seventy-five British and Irish authors, chosen by a person who is not you, resolved through redirects you did not have to notice, capped at five hundred revisions apiece for reasons that were explained to you rather than decided by you. Every technique in the second half of this course was demonstrated on that corpus. For the final project you build your own, and then run the semester over it.

You will define a population of Wikipedia pages, collect it through the MediaWiki and Wikimedia REST APIs, document it well enough that someone else could rebuild it, and analyze it with the temporal, textual, and network methods from Chapters 13 through 16. The analysis is the visible half of the project. The corpus is the half that decides whether the analysis means anything.

One thing to fix in your mind before you start, because it is the difference between a good project and a project that quietly lies. A Wikipedia page is not the thing it is about. The revision history of the Emily Brontë article is not a record of Emily Brontë; it is a record of what a few dozen volunteers did to a web page between 2003 and now. A link from one page to another was placed there by an editor in the last twenty years. A page view count measures readers, not importance. Every finding you report is, in the first instance, a finding about Wikipedia, and you will be asked to say what else it might be evidence of, and how confident you are in the leap.

The Corpus

Your corpus is between 75 and 250 article pages from a single Wikipedia language edition, and it is defined by a rule you write down, not by a set of pages you happened to like. Fewer than 75 and the vocabulary is too thin for TF-IDF to have anything to weigh; more than 250 and the collection runs long, the network plot becomes a hairball, and you spend the project waiting on the network instead of thinking.

There are three defensible ways to enumerate a population, all of them in Chapter 12. You can take the members of one or more categories, which is cheap and reproducible but inherits every quirk of volunteer tagging, including the authors who are simply missing the tag. You can curate a list by hand from an outside source, which gives you full control and forces you through the redirect machinery, because people typing 150 names make mistakes. You can query Wikidata for entities matching structured criteria and resolve the results to pages. Whichever you choose, the rule goes in the manifest, together with what it excludes.

The population must be coherent, in the sense that the pages are comparable to one another and it makes sense to ask what distinguishes them, and it must divide: you need at least one variable that splits the corpus into two or more groups of reasonable size, or you will have nothing to compare anything to.

Some populations are traps, and it is easier to say so now than to discover it in the middle of November. Corpora built out of bot-generated stubs (every asteroid, every village in a district, every species in a genus) will hand you two hundred pages of nearly identical text, an empty link network, and revision histories that record a script rather than a community. Corpora drawn from ongoing news events have page view series that are one spike and a flat line. Corpora where half the pages are three sentences long will not support text analysis of any kind. Check the length of a handful of your candidate pages before you commit.

Here are populations that work, offered as illustrations rather than a menu.

Population Why it works
The 118 chemical elements Exactly in range, dense link network, and every page has an external metadata sheet you can attach (atomic number, year of discovery, discoverer).
The 133 counties and independent cities of Virginia Divides on region and population; the link network is real; and the census gives you outside metadata that Wikipedia did not.
Winners of a major prize (Booker, Turing, Pulitzer, Ballon d'Or) Spans decades, so temporal questions have room; and the year of the award is a grouping variable with no Wikipedia in it.
Every film in a director's or studio's filmography, or the Best Picture nominees Page views respond to anniversaries and streaming releases; the link network connects films through the people who made them.
The Justices of the Supreme Court, or one country's cabinet ministers since 1945 Revision histories respond to news; the link network is dense and directed; appointing president and party come from outside.
Programming languages, or the video games nominated for a given award Strong link structure, and page views track the actual popularity of the thing, which you can check against a source that is not Wikipedia.

You may pick something not on this list, and most of you should. Bring it to the workshop and defend it.

The Tables

Your pipeline produces a directory of Parquet files and a manifest, in the two-layer structure of Chapter 12: the requests cache is the raw layer, the tables are the processed layer, and everything in the second can be rebuilt from the first without touching the network. At minimum you collect:

pages — the spine. One row per page, carrying page_id as the stable key and doc_id as the readable one, exactly as the authors table did. Every other table joins back to this one.

texts — one row per page, the plain-text extract. This feeds Chapters 14 and 15.

links — one row per outgoing internal link, in full. The filtered view that keeps only the edges inside your corpus is derived from it, not a substitute for it, because the network chapter may want to revisit that decision.

revisions — the edit history, with the revision ID, timestamp, user, size, and comment. Cap it at the 500 most recent revisions per page unless your corpus is small enough that the full history is cheap and a question actually needs it. Say which you did and why.

pageviews — daily views over a fixed window that you state, from the REST API. Some of your pages will have none, and the 404 is an answer, not an error.

langlinks — which other language editions have this page, and under what title.

Two metadata columns of your own, at minimum, that do not come from the API. The book's author corpus carries born, died, era, and gender, and Chapter 12 is blunt about where they came from: somebody compiled them by hand, and a reader who wants to check them has to check them against something other than Wikipedia. Do the same. Take them from a reference work, a database, an official record, or your own coding of each page, and record in the manifest where each one came from and how you resolved the ambiguous cases. Without them, every variable you can group by is one Wikipedia chose for you, and the project can only ever tell you what Wikipedia already thinks.

Collect politely: a real user agent, a cache, requests in series, and a pause between the ones that actually go over the network. You are one of about twenty-five people pointing a script at the same servers this month.

The Analysis

Six or more findings that answer research questions you posed in the proposal. Every finding gets a claim, the evidence, and one sentence saying what it is evidence of — a fact about the world, a fact about how Wikipedia is written, or something you cannot tell apart with the data you have. That third answer is a real finding and will be graded as one, as long as you say what you would have had to collect to separate them.

Across the notebook, and explicitly labeled where they appear, you must have:

  1. At least one finding from the revision histories and at least one from the page views, using the temporal tools of Chapter 13: a real date or datetime type, and at least one truncation, rolling window, or window function over time.
  2. At least one finding from the annotation table of Chapter 14: parts of speech, named entities, n-grams, keyword in context, or a complexity metric.
  3. At least one finding from the document vectors of Chapter 15: TF-IDF terms that distinguish your documents, and a distance or a UMAP projection of the collection with something interpretable said about its structure.
  4. At least one network, from Chapter 16, with the node and the edge defined in a sentence and defended in another. The internal link network is the obvious one; co-citation, directed, distance, and nearest-neighbor networks are all available from tables you already have. Report at least one centrality measure or clustering, and plot it.
  5. At least one finding that joins two of these layers. This is the one the book's corpus cannot give you and yours can. Do the pages that sit at the center of the link network get read more? Do the pages that were edited most in 2025 look different in vocabulary from the ones nobody touched? Does the group that your hand-coded variable splits out cluster in the UMAP projection, or does it not? Does the number of language editions a page appears in have anything to do with anything else you measured?

At least six plots, using at least four different geometries, of which at least one is faceted and at least one is fully labeled with labs.

The Datasheet

One page, written for somebody who has your Parquet files and none of your memories. What population does this corpus claim to represent, and by what rule. Who is in it who should not be, and who is missing. What was retrieved, from which endpoints, on what date, and what would change if it were retrieved again tomorrow. Which columns came from an API and which came from you, and how you decided the cases where you had to decide. What the corpus cannot be used for. What you would collect differently if you started over.

The name comes from the practice of publishing a datasheet alongside a dataset, in the way that an electronic component ships with a sheet stating its tolerances. Yours is a page of prose, not a form, and it is the deliverable that will still be worth something in five years.

Deliverables

  1. Proposal (Homework 44, due at Workshop I). The population and the rule that defines it, how you will enumerate it, roughly how many pages it holds, the two-or-more metadata columns you will add by hand and where they will come from, and three or four research questions with the tables each one needs, typed up as a single document. At the workshop you will trade proposals with a partner whose job is to break your population definition: to name a page that your rule lets in and should not, and one that it keeps out and should not. Populations are locked at the end of that class.
  2. The collection notebook. The pipeline, start to finish, from the population rule to the Parquet files. It must run. Do it in Colab and hand it in as a .py file (File → Download → Download .py).
  3. The corpus. The Parquet tables, the manifest, and the requests cache.
  4. The analysis notebook. Six or more findings, meeting the requirements above. Like the collection notebook, hand it in as a .py file downloaded from Colab.
  5. The datasheet. One page, typed.
  6. Presentation. A pre-recorded video of six to eight minutes. There are no slides required and it does not need to be formal, but it should really walk us through what you found: your population and how you defined it, one network plot, one temporal plot, and a clear account of your main findings — what you learned about your subject, what you learned about Wikipedia, and how confident you are in telling those two apart. See Submitting Your Work for how to record and hand it in.

Grading

  • Proposal and population definition — 15%
  • The corpus and the pipeline — 30%
  • Analysis notebook — 35%
  • Datasheet — 10%
  • Presentation — 10%

The corpus is graded on the care of its definition and the honesty of its documentation, not on how exciting its subject is. A modest population, cleanly enumerated, fully keyed, and frankly described, beats an ambitious one whose foreign keys do not resolve and whose manifest cannot say where half the columns came from.
