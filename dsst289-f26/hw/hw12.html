Assume you have three datasets loaded into Python. The first, students, has one row for each student in the class, with columns named name, major, year, gpa, and study_hours. The second, majors, has one row for each major offered by the university, with columns named major, department, and n_faculty. The third, roommates, has one row for each pair of students who live together, with columns named name and roommate, both of which hold student names. Hand write the following.

  1. A primary key for each of the three datasets.
  2. A list of every foreign key found in the three datasets. For each one, name the table it appears in and the table whose primary key it refers to.
  3. Suppose we want to add each student's department to the students dataset by joining it with majors, but a few students have declared a major that does not appear in the majors table. Describe, in words, what the default join does with those students and what a left join does with them instead. If the goal is to count the students in each department, say which of the two you would use and why.
  4. We want a table showing which pairs of roommates share a major. Describe, in words, the steps needed to build it. Your description should say how many times students has to be joined to roommates, which column of roommates each join matches on, why the second join needs a suffix, and what the final step compares.

Solutions

1. A primary key for each dataset.

students:  name
majors:    major
roommates: name and roommate together

A student who lives with two people appears in two rows of roommates, so name on its own does not identify a row, and the same is true of roommate. The pair of columns taken together does, because the same two students are not listed together twice.

2. Every foreign key, with the table it appears in and the table it refers to.

major     in students   refers to majors
name      in roommates  refers to students
roommate  in roommates  refers to students

Whether a column is a primary key or a foreign key depends on the table it sits in. In majors, major identifies a row, so it is the primary key there. In students, many rows can share the same major, so there it is a foreign key pointing back at majors.

3. What the default join and a left join do with students whose major is missing from majors, and which to use when counting students in each department.

The default is an inner join. It keeps only the rows that match in both tables, so those students disappear from the result with no warning. A left join keeps every row of students and fills in department and n_faculty with null for the students who have no match. For counting students by department, the left join is the safer choice: the counts then add up to the number of students in the class, and the students with an unknown department show up as their own null group instead of silently vanishing from the total.

4. The steps needed to build a table of roommate pairs who share a major.

students has to be joined to roommates twice, because each row of roommates refers to two different students. The first join matches the name column of roommates to the name column of students and brings in the first student's major. The second join matches the roommate column of roommates to the name column of students and brings in the second student's major. Both joins add a column called major, so the second join needs a suffix, such as _roommate, to keep the two columns apart. The final step filters the rows to those where the two major columns are equal. Selecting only name and major from students before joining keeps the other student columns from being copied in twice.
