Homework 15

[← back]

Due at Project Workshop I, together with Homework 16. This is the first of two homeworks that together make up the proposal for the final project. Read the project description before you start. Everything below concerns the dataset you will collect about your own two weeks. Unlike the other homeworks, type your answers rather than hand writing them, so that they can be submitted through Box as part of your proposal. Bring a copy to class as well, since you will work from it in the workshop.

  1. Two or three research questions that you want your two weeks of data to answer. Each one should be a question you genuinely do not know the answer to, and each one should be answerable by comparing numbers, not just by counting things. Under each question, write the one sentence you would need to be able to say at the end of the project in order to have answered it.
  2. The measurements. Your hours table must carry at least two numeric measures that you invent and record about yourself each hour. List yours, and for each one give its units, its range of possible values, and a one-sentence definition precise enough that you will still apply it the same way on day thirteen as on day one. Then say which of your research questions each measure serves. If a measure serves none of them, cut it.
  3. The tables. List every table you plan to collect, and for each one give its name, its unit of observation, and the number of rows you expect it to have when the collection is finished. You need hours, places, activities, and courses at a minimum, plus at least one table of your own design that exists only because one of your research questions needs it. Say in a sentence why that last table is a separate table rather than extra columns on an existing one.
  4. The columns. For every table, list every column with its name, its type, and its units or its expected set of values. Check every name against the naming rules from the reading. Mark the column that uniquely identifies each row of the table (its primary key), and mark every column whose values point at the rows of another table (a foreign key), along with the table it points to. These two terms get a whole chapter of their own when we reach joins later in the semester; for now the plain-language versions are all you need.
  5. The relational diagram. Draw all of your tables as boxes, with an arrow running from each foreign key to the primary key it points to. Include the weather table that will be provided to you, and draw the key that connects it to hours.
  6. The collection plan. Say how you will actually record an hour: on your phone, on paper, in a spreadsheet, at the top of every hour, or reconstructed at the end of the day. Then name the two hours of your typical day that you are most likely to fail to record, and say what you will do about them.
